Field note 01 / Sales & human behavior

The work before the pitch.

A better sales conversation starts with understanding what someone is trying to protect, change, or get done.

Carlo Doroff4 min read · 750 words

In this essay

Door-to-door sales makes one thing very clear: someone can understand every word of your pitch and still have no reason to buy. Attention is borrowed. Trust has to be earned. A polished explanation is useful, but it cannot substitute for a problem that matters to the person standing in front of you. That distinction is easy to forget when you spend more time preparing a presentation than listening to a customer.

During the summer of 2025, I worked in door-to-door sales at EcoShield and generated more than $107,000 in contract value. The number belongs on a résumé. The more interesting question belongs in a notebook: what makes a conversation useful enough for someone to continue it? That question connects my sales experience to building Craze and, now, working on go-to-market at Morsa.

Start with the situation

A product description begins with what you have. Discovery begins with what another person is dealing with. Those are different starting points, and they lead to different conversations. If I begin by listing features, I am asking someone to translate my offering into their life. If I begin by understanding the situation, I take responsibility for making that connection myself.

A useful question is specific enough to produce an example. “Would this be helpful?” invites a polite answer. “How did you handle this the last time it happened?” invites a story about an actual decision. That story can reveal the workaround, the people involved, the cost of doing nothing, and the reason a previous solution did not stick. It gives the conversation something concrete to work with.

An objection contains information

It is tempting to treat objections as hurdles in a memorized sequence. Too expensive: explain the value. Too busy: create urgency. Already have a provider: explain the difference. Preparation helps, but an automatic response can miss what the person actually means. The same sentence can describe a budget limit, a lack of trust, a bad prior experience, or a problem that simply is not important enough.

I prefer to think of an objection as a request for a better understanding. Before answering it, I need to know which uncertainty is underneath it. Sometimes that means asking a follow-up question. Sometimes it means accepting that the fit is weak. A conversation that ends with an honest disqualification can still be a good use of time. It leaves both people with a clearer picture of the situation.

Respect changes the quality of the conversation

There is a practical reason to be direct about what a product can and cannot do. A sale creates expectations that someone has to fulfill later. If the promise is vague or inflated, the work does not disappear when the contract is signed. It moves to onboarding, support, or the next renewal conversation. The person making the promise should think about that downstream experience.

This is especially relevant when selling something technical. An AI product can sound impressive while leaving the buyer unsure about the most basic things: where it fits, who uses it, and how they will know it worked. Clear language is part of the product experience. Explaining the limits can make the useful part easier to understand. It also gives a customer a better basis for deciding whether to move forward.

Build a feedback loop, not just a pipeline

At Morsa, my work includes translating customer conversations into structured feedback for the founding team. That creates a bridge between a commercial conversation and a product decision. The useful record is more than whether a prospect said yes or no. It includes the workflow they described, the constraint they mentioned, and the evidence they would need before changing how they work.

The same discipline matters in any early-stage business. A pipeline tells you where opportunities stand. A learning process tells you what those opportunities are teaching you. Both are necessary. Without the second, a team can repeat the same assumptions with greater efficiency and mistake the activity for progress.

What I want to carry forward

My goal is to become better at the work that happens before persuasion: paying attention, asking a precise question, and making a fair assessment of fit. Selling is a chance to understand another person's priorities under real constraints. When that understanding is strong, the pitch becomes simpler. You can explain the relevant part, acknowledge the tradeoffs, and agree on a next step that makes sense to both sides.

That is the kind of commercial work I want to keep doing. The outcome matters, and so does the quality of the reasoning that gets you there. A good conversation should leave the customer more informed and the builder less certain about the wrong things.

Have a different perspective? I’d like to hear it.
